Tankless water heaters use high-powered burners to rapidly heat water as it flows through a heat exchanger. When a hot water tap is turned on, cold water travels through a pipe into the unit, where it’s heated by either a gas burner or electric elements. This process ensures a constant supply of hot water without the limitations of a storage tank. Some of the benefits include:

Energy efficiency: According to the U.S. Department of Energy, tankless units can be 24%–34% more efficient than conventional storage tank water heaters.

Space savings: These compact units take up significantly less space than traditional tank-style heaters.

Longevity: Tankless water heaters typically last longer than conventional units, with a lifespan of 20 years or more. Tank water heaters generally have a lifespan of around 12 years.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey, This Old House plumbing and heating expert, explains the core advantage: “Condensing tankless water heaters are so efficient, they’re certified by the federal Energy Star program, making them eligible for utility rebates across the country. These rebates are often enough to bridge the difference in price between the more expensive condensing units and the cheaper noncondensing ones. Then it’s basically a free or low-cost upgrade that will save money for the next 20 years or more.”

The Knepley family of central Pennsylvania experienced the limitations of tank-style heaters firsthand. With four young children, “bath time in our household is like an assembly line,” says Rebecca Knepley. The successive filling of the tub for each child would empty their 50-gallon hot-water tank by bath number three—an unfortunate predicament for the occupant of bath number four. After replacing their electric tank with a propane on-demand heater in 1999, the family never ran out of hot water again.

Evaluate your household’s hot water needs to determine the appropriate size and capacity of the tankless unit. Consider factors such as the number of bathrooms, appliances that use hot water, and peak usage times.

Select an appropriate location for your tankless water heater. Tankless units can be installed indoors or outdoors, depending on the model and local climate. The chosen spot should allow for proper ventilation and easy access for maintenance.

This Old House plumbing and heating expert Richard Trethewey cautions that tankless heaters aren’t for every household: “In a household with lots of simultaneous water use—say, multiple showers running at the same time as the dishwasher or washing machine—a tankless heater may not be able to keep up with demand.” Understanding your peak demand in gallons per minute is essential before selecting a unit.

Proper electrical and gas connections are crucial for safely operating your tankless water heater. For electric models, ensure the unit is connected to an appropriately sized circuit breaker and wiring. Gas models require gas line sizing and pressure that meets the unit’s demands.

Proper venting is also essential for gas-powered tankless water heaters to ensure safe operation and efficient performance. The manufacturer should provide instructions on the venting requirements of the model you choose.

Once the tankless water heater is installed and connected, adjust it for optimal performance. Most tankless water heaters allow you to set the desired water temperature. The recommended setting is typically between 120°F and 140°F, balancing comfort, safety, and energy efficiency.

Modern tankless units often feature digital control panels allowing precise temperature control and programming. Familiarize yourself with the control panel and set it according to your household’s needs.

For gas models, getting the gas line right is critical. According to This Old House Magazine, the diameter of the supply pipe often has to be increased to ¾ inch for the burner to perform properly, delivering enough volume at sufficient pressure. If the pressure falls short, the gas company will have to adjust the regulator on the meter. Some tankless units, however, are designed to work with a standard ½-inch gas line, provided it isn’t longer than 24 feet.

Tankless Water Heater Testing and Maintenance

Once your new tankless water heater is installed, you can test the system. Turn on the water supply and check for leaks. For gas models, turn on the gas supply and check for gas leaks using a soapy water solution. Finally, turn on the power to the unit and test hot water at various fixtures.

Your new system will last longer and work more effectively if you also perform the following regular maintenance tasks:

Flush the system annually to remove mineral buildup.

Clean or replace air intake filters as needed.

Inspect venting systems for obstructions or damage.
