When you’re choosing a bathroom exhaust fan, there are two main options: a standard bathroom vent fan or a remote vent fan. Eastman shows these fan options to the homeowner, who chooses the quieter, less obtrusive remote fan. Standard Bathroom Vent Fan

A standard bathroom vent fan is the most common type. It features a motor installed directly behind the grill in the bathroom ceiling. Expect a standard bathroom vent fan to have these characteristics: Bulkier appearance Easier to install More affordable Louder during operation Remote Fan

One key advantage of a remote fan is that the grill in the bathroom ceiling contains no electronics—just a grill and dampers. As demonstrated on a recent This Old House project, this means the grill can be installed anywhere in the bathroom, including inside the shower or water closet, since there’s no motor or wiring in the wet area. The motor is then placed wherever it’s most accessible—in the attic, basement, or another convenient location—and connected to the grill via ductwork.

Remote fans offer a different approach to bathroom ventilation: Connected to the bathroom via ductwork Generally quieter than standard fans

For the ductwork connecting the fan to the exterior, This Old House projects typically use flexible insulated ducting. The insulation serves double duty: it dampens noise for quieter operation and prevents condensation from forming when warm, moist bathroom air passes through unconditioned spaces like the attic. Venting can be routed out through a gable end or through the roof, though going through a sidewall like a gable end is generally the easier path. Assessing Your Bathroom Space

Before installing a bathroom fan, evaluate your space and access points. What may work perfectly for one home will be wrong for another, so look at your bathroom, ceiling access, and attic carefully to ensure there’s room for a bathroom fan installation. Eastman emphasizes the importance of this step.

Pay attention to these areas in particular:

Ceiling access: You’ll need access to the ceiling from either the bathroom itself or the attic space above. Plus, you need enough room for the fan unit and direct access to the exterior of your home for venting.

Attic: If possible, inspect the attic space above the bathroom for open areas for fan placement, existing ductwork or venting options, and potential obstructions like plumbing or electrical lines. Choosing a Bathroom Fan Location

The positioning of your bathroom fan will affect how well the fan removes moisture and steam from the bathroom, thereby reducing the risk of mold and mildew.

A good spot for an exhaust fan is above or within one foot of the shower. This position allows the fan to capture steam and moisture at the source, prevent humidity from spreading throughout the bathroom, and reduce the risk of mold and mildew growth.

While above or near the shower is preferred, fans can be installed in other spots in the bathroom. Factor in ceiling joists, structural elements, and existing electrical wiring to find a location for the fan that ideally doesn’t require moving systems around. Also, consider aesthetics and room layout to find a location that is the least obtrusive.

Remote fans offer more flexibility in grill placement, as they can pull air from various locations within the room. This means you could have the fan intake near the shower but the motor located in a less obtrusive location, like the attic.

There are several switch options for controlling your new bathroom fan:

Humidity sensor switch: This switch automatically activates the fan when the humidity level in the bathroom rises, helping prevent mold and mildew by ensuring proper ventilation without manual operation.

Standard on/off switch: A basic, manual switch allows you to turn the fan on or off as needed. It offers simple control but requires you to remember to turn the fan off after use.

Installing a bathroom fan, especially in an older home, involves several steps. Eastman demonstrates how to install the fan from start to finish. He starts by turning off the power to the bathroom at the circuit breaker. To prepare the space, remove existing fixtures or cut a new opening for the fan. Wiring the Fan

To wire the fan, you first need to locate a suitable power source. Finding a power source might be challenging in an older home, but safely running new wiring is essential for the fan’s operation. Eastman demonstrates how to fish new wiring through the walls when dealing with outdated electrical systems.

Eastman starts by sourcing an access point to fish the new wire for the remote motor. He drills a hole that will run parallel to the plumbing chase from the basement to the attic, then goes up to the attic and does the same while staying as in line as possible. Next, he drops a fiberglass fish down from the attic and catches it in the basement. He ties the new wire on and then pulls it back up to the attic. He repeats the same process to wire in the bathroom fan switch. Then it’s time to connect the fan to the chosen switch type.

Mounting the Fan and Connecting Ductwork

Mount the fan securely and install ductwork to prevent moisture from escaping into other parts of your home. To install the grill in the homeowner’s shower, Eastman cuts a hole in the ceiling using a six-inch hole saw. After the damper has dropped into place, he secures it to the joist using wood screws. With the remote motor mounted and wired, all that’s left to do is connect the ductwork and run it to the exterior of the gable lamp. Eastman collaborated with a sheet metal technician to install 4-inch ductwork from the fan to the exterior of the gable end. Proper external venting prevents moisture buildup in the attic or walls.

When venting the ductwork to the exterior, Eastman prefers going out through a side wall rather than through the roof whenever possible. To ensure a watertight seal, he applies silicone to the outside of the vent before sliding it into place and screwing it to the house, then installs a grill over the opening to keep animals from getting inside. Finally, he places insulation back around the fan to keep warm air in the bathroom—a small but important step that’s easy to overlook.

Choosing the right ductwork for your installation needs can make a big difference in both performance and noise levels. Flexible insulated ductwork is ideal for short, straight runs, while rigid metal ducts are best for longer runs or when making turns. Bathroom exhaust fans must be vented to the outside of your home, commonly through the roof, a gable end, or an exterior wall. Finishing Touches

Testing the fan before sealing all access points lets you troubleshoot any issues before they become big problems. You might experience unusual noise or poor suction, but sealing all the gaps around the fan grill should ensure that all vented air is directed outside.

Additional Fan Installation Tips for Older Homes

Older homes come with their own unique challenges and quirks. Here are some additional tips for successfully installing a bathroom fan in an older property. Evaluating Existing Wiring

Older homes often have outdated wiring types that might not be compatible with modern appliances. When you encounter BX wire or knob-and-tube wiring, consider updating these systems not just for the bathroom fan but for overall electrical safety. Attic and Crawl Space Safety

Inspecting attics and crawl spaces in older homes can reveal unexpected hazards, such as old asbestos insulation or structural issues. Wear appropriate protective gear and ensure stable footing. Consulting Professionals

While DIY projects offer a sense of accomplishment, consulting with an electrician for the wiring aspects and a contractor for venting could save time and help you avoid potential issues. Their expertise is particularly valuable in older homes with unpredictable conditions. Benefits of Proper Bathroom Ventilation

Installing a bathroom fan reduces humidity and moisture buildup, which prevents mold and mildew growth and protects bathroom fixtures and finishes from moisture damage.
