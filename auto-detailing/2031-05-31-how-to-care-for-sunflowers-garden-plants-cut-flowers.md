The sunflower is a beautiful plant that adds cheer to any garden, houseplant collection, or bouquet. If you’re curious how to help these sunny flowers thrive—whether they’re already growing in your yard, you want to start them from seed, or you have some fresh cut in a vase— we’re here to help! In this article, we’ll explain how to care for sunflower plants (mature plants and seeds) and fresh cut sunflowers, with wikiHow-exclusive advice from plant experts and florists. Sunflower Care Guide

Growing and mature sunflower plants need 6-8 hours of direct sunlight each day. Water them about once per week (or whenever the soil feels dry) and ensure they’re planted in well-draining soil that doesn’t get soggy. Sunflowers don’t usually need fertilizer and typically only need protection from hungry deer and birds. Steps Section 1 of 4: Caring for a Sunflower Plant Download Article 1

Give your sunflowers 6 to 8 hours of direct sunlight. Given their name and the fact that they follow the sun’s path, sunflowers love lots of sunlight. Whether you’re growing your plants in the ground or in pots, ensure you put them in a spot that gets full sun for 6 to 8 hours each day so they stand tall and keep thriving.[1]XResearch source Meet the wikiHow Experts

Pilar Zuniga is a floral designer and the owner of Gorgeous and Green, a floral design studio, with over 10 years of experience.

Denise Porcaro Tupper is a floral design expert and the founder and owner of Flower Girl NYC. She has over 20 years of experience and specializes in curating bouquets. 2

Water your sunflowers about once per week. Once your sunflower has developed a deep taproot and several leaves, it’s actually fairly drought-resistant. It’ll still thrive on regular, deep watering, especially while buds and flowers are developing, so simply wait for the soil to dry out between watering sessions. In general, mature sunflowers need about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water per week.[2]XResearch source

Over-watering is more likely to harm sunflowers than under-watering them.

Try not to get the blooms wet, as this can damage them. Advertisement 3

Ensure the soil your sunflower is in is well-draining. Sunflowers aren’t too picky about the soil they’re grown in, as they can tolerate lots of different types. However, they do best in well-draining soil that doesn’t stay soggy. Just make sure the place you planted them in or the soil you added to their pot doesn’t develop puddles or get waterlogged.[3]XResearch source

If the sunflower’s soil isn’t well-draining, move them to a spot that stays relatively dry in your garden, or repot them using well-draining potting soil.

While sunflowers do grow well in a variety of soils, they tend to prefer slightly acidic (6.0 to 6.8 pH), sandy loam soils.[4]XResearch source 4

Fertilize the sunflowers if the soil is poor or to speed up growth. Sunflowers don’t really need fertilizer and grow well on their own. However, if your soil is poor and lacking nutrients, or you simply want the plant to grow faster and develop bigger blooms, fertilizer can help. Once your plant has several sets of leaves, apply a balanced, slow-release fertilizer to the soil.[5]XResearch source

Follow the directions on the package of fertilizer for how often to fertilize your plant. Most slow-release fertilizers only need to be reapplied every few weeks to months.

Avoid overfertilizing your sunflowers and using fertilizer high in nitrogen, as this can lead to poor flower formation.[6]XResearch source It may also cause your plant to grow leggy. 5

Protect sunflowers from pests, like deer and birds. Deer are some of the most notorious sunflower pests and can eat the plants quickly. If you live in an area with lots of deer, protect your sunflowers with wire fencing. Birds can also destroy sunflowers, as they’re attracted to the seeds. Luckily, putting bird spinners, owl decoys, or even scarecrows in your garden can help keep them away.[7]XResearch source

Sunflowers aren’t too prone to insect pests. They can occasionally attract caterpillars, aphids, stink bugs, and sunflower headclipping weevils, but they don’t really need much management.[8]XResearch source

If you notice your plant is being destroyed by insects, apply an organic insecticide that’s safe for pollinators, as sunflowers attract lots of beneficial insects, too!

Slugs can sometimes be a nuisance, too. To keep slugs away, surround your plants with slug repellent from a garden store, or make your own "beer traps" for slugs to fall in.[9]XResearch source 6

Look out for shrunken yellow or white leaves. Downy mildew and powdery mildew can be a problem in waterlogged soils or very humid and wet areas. Check regularly for either fungi, which causes shrunken yellow leaves (downy) and white spots on the leaves (downy and powdery). If you see these symptoms, reduce watering to keep soil dry, and apply a fungicide. Infected plants rarely produce flowers, so you may want to simply remove them to avoid spreading the disease.[10]XResearch source

To help prevent mildew, avoid getting the sunflower’s leaves wet during waterings. If you’re growing multiple plants, ensure they’re spaced about 1 to 2 feet (30.5-61 cm) apart.

If the leaves look normal except for a striking yellow bleached color, this may be a bacterial infection instead. Keeping the soil dry may help here as well, and the plant will usually be fine.[11]XResearch source

If the leaf looks yellow but still has green veins, the problem may be a nitrogen deficiency or indicate that you’re overwatering your sunflower. Applying a nitrogen-rich fertilizer and cutting back on watering can help.[12]XResearch source

Be aware that it’s normal for sunflowers to lose their first set of leaves once they begin growing in earnest. Don’t worry if the bottom leaves yellow and drop, but the rest of the leaves are fine. 7

Stake the sunflowers in windy weather. Wind can severely damage most multi-branched varieties, as well as any sunflower that’s over 3 feet (91.4 cm) tall. Simply tie the stems to a sturdy support, like a stake or durable stick, using twist ties, velcro, tape, or a soft cloth.[13]XResearch source

You may need to put up a wind barrier for extra tall sunflowers. Or, if you’re growing your plants in pots, move them to an area that’s sheltered from the wind. Advertisement Section 2 of 4: Planting and Growing Sunflower Seeds Download Article 1

Choose a sunflower variety to grow. Sunflowers range from 16-foot (4.9 m) stalks to knee-high clusters of pom-poms. So, choose a variety you like depending on the size of your space, if you want to grow your flowers in pots, and what kind of blooms you like best. If you want to grow and keep your sunflower in a pot, experts at the plant nursery and garden shop Artemisia Nursery definitely recommend getting a dwarf variety.[14]XExpert SourceArtemisia Nursery

Here are some popular varieties to consider:[15]XResearch source

Giant sunflower varieties: American Giant Hybrid and the Mammoth Grey Stripe

Dwarf sunflower varieties: Sunspot and Big Smile

Branching varieties (produce multiple blooms): Autumn Beauty and Moulin Rouge

[for Sunflowers](https://for-sunflowers.northlist.xyz/for-sunflowers/20260826.html)

Here are some other considerations to take when choosing a variety:

The classic, yellow, single-stem sunflowers usually grow quickly and easily. Sunrich and Pro Cut are good beginner options.

Branching varieties tend to grow more slowly. If you plan to cut the flowers, get a pollen-free or low-pollen variety like the Cherry Rose.

Giant varieties tend to produce tastier seeds and can provide support for climbing plants, such as the Mammoth Gray Stripe and Humongous. 2

Start the seeds in the spring or summer once the threat of frost is past. You can plant sunflower seeds starting in the spring, once the last frost is past, and into late summer. If you’re planting them directly in the soil, just make sure it’s warmed up to around 50°F (10°C).[16]XTrustworthy SourceIowa State University Extension and OutreachIowa State University's Extension and Outreach program dedicated to educating and engaging communitiesGo to source If you want to start the seeds early while it’s still cold out, plant them in pots about 2 to 3 weeks before the last frost.[17]XResearch source

A note on when to start seeds: Some sunflower varieties are daylight sensitive and might not flower if they’re planted too late. These sunflowers, called long-day sunflowers, grow stems and leaves while the days are still short (spring) and bloom as the daylight increases (summer).[18]XResearch source

However, there are also day-neutral varieties that can be planted at any time during the growing season.

Plan ahead based on the expected flowering time. On average, most sunflowers bloom around 60 days after planting from seed.[19]XResearch source However, some branched sunflower varieties bloom after 90 days. 3

Find a spot with well-drained soil and full sun, or plant the seeds in pots. If you want to start the seeds directly in the ground, sunflowers grow best in well-draining soil (i.e., doesn’t get soggy) and need 6 to 8 hours of direct sun.[20]XResearch source You can also plant the seeds in pots, if you prefer.

If you’re planting the seeds in a pot, fill a container that has drainage holes with well-draining potting mix. Select a pot size depending on the variety you’re growing; most dwarf varieties need a pot that’s at least 1 foot (30.5 cm) wide.

If you want to start your seeds in pots but transplant them in your garden, fill biodegradable containers with well-draining soil. Choosing a biodegradable container means you can simply plant the entire pot in the soil without digging out your plant![21]XResearch source

If the soil is poor, mix in 3 or 4 inches (7.6-10.2 cm) of compost into the topsoil.[22]XResearch source 4

Plant the seeds 1 to 2 inches (2.5-5 cm) deep in the soil. In general, large, single-stem sunflowers need to be planted at least 1 inch (2.5 cm) deep into the soil.[23]XResearch source However, if you have very good quality soil (not too dry or sandy), you can likely plant the seeds ½ inch (1.3 cm) deep, instead.[24]XResearch source

Plant extra seeds if you have the space. You may thin them later, or lose some seeds to pests. 5

Space the seeds in the ground depending on the size of the plant. If you’re planting the seeds directly in the ground, it’s important to space the plants away from each other so they grow properly. How far you need to space the seeds depends on the variety you’re growing:[25]XResearch source

Dwarf varieties (2-5 ft or 61-152.4 cm tall): Space the seeds about 6 inches (15.2 cm) apart.

Tall varieties (5+ ft or 152.4 cm tall): Space the seeds about 1 foot (30.5 cm) apart.

Giant varieties (7+ ft or 213.4 cm tall): Space the seeds about 2 feet (61 cm) apart.

Branching varieties: Space the seeds about 18 inches (45.7 cm) apart. 6

Water the soil regularly so the soil stays moist. Sunflower seeds need plenty of water to germinate, grow roots, and establish themselves. Keep the soil moist, but not soaked, until the seedlings emerge. This usually happens within 5 to 10 days of planting them, but may take longer in cold weather.[26]XResearch source

Once the seedling emerges from the soil, cut back on waterings. Just water your new sunflower when the soil feels dry (which is usually once per week). 7

Transplant pot-grown seedlings when they have a second set of leaves. If you started your sunflower seeds indoors and want to transplant them into your garden, Artemisia Nursery says to wait until it gets its first set of true leaves. These are the 2 leaves that grow after the seedling’s first set of leaves, which are its seed leaves.[27]XExpert SourceArtemisia Nursery

Plant Nursery & Garden ShopExpert Interview

To transplant the seedlings, simply dig a hole in your garden that’s as deep and wide as the pot. If you grew the seeds in biodegradable pots, simply place the pot inside. Otherwise, gently remove the seedling from the pot and place it in the hole. Advertisement Section 3 of 4: Caring for Cut Sunflowers Download Article 1

Fill a clean vase with fresh water. When caring for any type of cut flower, florist Pilar Zuniga says it’s imperative to wash out your vase with soap and water so it’s clean. Let it dry out, then fill it with fresh water.[28]XExpert SourcePilar Zuniga FloristExpert Interview 2

Cut the ends of the stems at a 45° angle. Use a pair of sharp, clean scissors or cutting shears to snip off the bottom ½ inch (1.3 cm) of each stem at a 45° angle. This provides more surface area for the flower to absorb water. Plus, it keeps the bottom of the stem off the bottom of the vase (which also helps with water absorption).[29]XResearch source 3

Remove any leaves that will be submerged in the water. Leaving any leaves submerged in the water can cause them to rot and spoil the water, which can prevent your cut sunflowers from lasting as long. Simply use scissors or your fingers to take off any leaves that will sit below the water line.[30]XResearch source 4

Set your sunflowers in a cool spot that gets filtered light. According to florist Jeanne Walker, cut flowers last the longest when they’re kept in a relatively cool, dark place. She suggests placing them in a spot that gets filtered light rather than direct light (like a windowsill), as lots of light can cause the flowers to continue to open.[31]XExpert SourceJeanne Walker FloristExpert Interview 5

Add a floral preservative to the water. This is another great way to keep your sunflowers fresh for longer, as preservatives are full of nutrients that keep your flowers looking tall and happy. Simply read the directions on the preservative for how much to add to the water.[32]XResearch source

Alternatively, Walker suggests adding a drop of bleach into the water. This helps slow down the bacterial growth that eventually spoils your flowers.[33]XExpert SourceJeanne Walker

FloristExpert Interview Plus, bleach may be more effective for sunflowers than commercial preservatives![34]XTrustworthy SourceUniversity of California Division of Agriculture and Natural ResourcesExtension program of the University of California system devoted to educating and improving local communitiesGo to source 6

Change out the water every 1 to 2 days. Walker recommends giving cut flowers fresh water each day; while it’s a bit of work, it helps preserve the flowers and make them last longer. An easy trick is to run cool water on one side of the vase so the old water flushes out on the other, she says.[35]XExpert SourceJeanne Walker

FloristExpert Interview Alternatively, you can grab the stems of the sunflowers together and dump out the water. 7

Snip the ends of the stems each time you change the water. Whenever you add fresh water to the vase, floral design expert Denise Porcaro Tupper suggests giving all the stems a fresh cut across. Aim to cut all of the stems again at an angle, taking about ¼ to ½ inch (0.6-1.3 cm) off at the end of the stem.[36]XExpert SourceDenise Porcaro Tupper

Floral Design ExpertExpert Interview This helps the flowers to absorb water well.

Check that no leaves or other foliage are in the water when you cut the stems, Tupper says. If you see anything, remove it from the water.[37]XExpert SourceDenise Porcaro Tupper Floral Design ExpertExpert Interview 8

Remove dying or dead flowers from the vase. A “bad” flower can spoil the rest of the flowers in your vase, Tupper notes, as they introduce more bacteria to the water. So, if any of your flowers start to die off, simply take them out of the vase.[38]XExpert SourceDenise Porcaro Tupper Floral Design ExpertExpert Interview Advertisement Section 4 of 4: Harvesting Flowers and Seeds Download Article 1

Cut decorative flowers when they are ¼ open. At this stage, the flower is just starting to open and the petals stick up perpendicular to the central disc. Cut them at this point for maximum vase life, which is usually around 5 to 7 days.[39]XResearch source

Cut the flowers in the early morning or early evening. Use clean shears or a knife.

Strip off any leaves that will be below the water line in the vase.

Place the cut flowers in water immediately. 2

Cut flowers for drying when they’re about half open. In this case, it's best to wait until the flower is about half open, and the petals are leaning outward. Once cut, there are many ways to preserve them. The simplest is to tie the stems with twine and hang them upside down in a warm room with good ventilation.[40]XResearch source 3

Harvest the seed head. If you'd like to harvest the sunflowers’ seeds yourself, cut off the seed head once the disk it rests on starts to turn from green to yellow. Store the head in a dry, cool location until the disk turns dark brown. The seeds are now ready to roast and eat!

Store seeds in a cloth bag to allow air to circulate. This helps prevent mold.

Sunflowers attract other animals who want the seeds, too. Once the flowers start to droop and lose their petals, cover the head with cheesecloth or a paper bag to keep the seeds protected from animals.

To care for sunflowers, plant them 1 inch deep, after the last frost, and in loamy soil with good drainage and full sun. Keep the soil moist as they are establishing themselves, but let the soil dry out between waterings once they're mature. If you want to harvest the flowers to keep in a vase, cut them when the petals are perpendicular to the central disk so they will last longer. Alternatively, for seed saving, cut them when the petals are leaning outward. Keep reading for tips from our Horticulturist reviewer on how to protect your sunflowers from pests!
